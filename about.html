About

A short story

I started in chemistry. Not because I planned to, but because it was the subject I could sit with for hours without noticing. That lasted through my BSc, until I discovered that the problems I found most interesting — how populations of neurons encode something as ordinary as a repeated image — were not really chemistry problems at all.

So I moved. Slowly at first, then more deliberately, into computational neuroscience. I completed certified training through Neuromatch Academy and wrote a thesis on how neural representations reorganize and stabilize when the same visual experience is repeated. It was a small study, but it taught me what real computational work feels like: messy data, ambiguous results, and the constant temptation to over-interpret.

From there, the path has been less a straight line than a gradual widening. I now work across machine learning for biomedical and clinical data — single-cell transcriptomics, clinical prediction, biomedical language models — and I build the software infrastructure that makes those models testable, reusable, and honest about their limits.

What connects all of it is a single question I keep returning to: how do we know a model is actually right? Not just accurate on a benchmark — right in the ways that matter. That question shapes how I evaluate models, how I document them, and how I write the code around them.

I'm currently looking for a PhD position where I can pursue this question seriously — in a group that takes model reliability, reproducibility, and biomedical applications as real problems, not afterthoughts.
